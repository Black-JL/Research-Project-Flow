# Orwell — "Politics and the English Language" (*Horizon*, April 1946)

Quotations checked against the text published by the Orwell Foundation:
https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/

Use this as a **guideline**, not a voice. Orwell sets out how to find the plain word. He is not a style to imitate.

## The six rules

1. "Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print."
2. "Never use a long word where a short one will do."
3. "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out."
4. "Never use the passive where you can use the active."
5. "Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent."
6. "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous."

Rule 5 needs care in research prose. A technical term is fine when it is the precise name of the thing ("difference-in-differences", "standard error") and the reader knows it. It is jargon when an everyday word would carry the same meaning, or when the reader doesn't know it.

## The questions to ask of every sentence

"What am I trying to say?" "What words will express it?" "What image or idiom will make it clearer?" "Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?" Then two more: "Could I put it more shortly?" and "Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?"

## The core instruction

"What is above all needed is to let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about."

This is the rule AI prose breaks most. A language model picks the phrase that usually comes next, and Orwell describes the result: "phrases tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house."

## The four habits to hunt for

| Orwell's name | What it looks like in research prose | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dying metaphors | "sheds light on", "a double-edged sword", "the tip of the iceberg", "paves the way" | Say the literal thing. |
| Operators, or verbal false limbs | "plays a role in", "has an impact on", "serves to", "is subject to", "give rise to" | Use the single verb: "affects", "raises", "causes". |
| Pretentious diction | "utilize", "facilitate", "leverage", "paradigm", "landscape", "multifaceted" | Use the short, plain word. |
| Meaningless words | "significant" (outside statistics), "robust" (outside statistics), "important", "key", "nuanced" | Name the size or the reason, or cut the word. |
