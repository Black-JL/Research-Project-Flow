# PEER paragraphs, the one-point rule, and the skip test

Source: David Stuckler's writing videos (Fast Track Grad), transcribed 2026-10-19:
- "Fastest Way To Improve Your Academic Writing (PEER System)": https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gFv47hloKN8
- "How To Improve Your Academic Writing Immediately (3 Tips)": https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7FrYXvJOO44
- "Why ChatGPT Won't Fix Your Academic Writing (3 REAL Fixes)": https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8zVYCCTu3ow

This is a **structure** guideline. It says how a paragraph is built, not how it sounds.

## One-point rule

Each paragraph makes one point, no more and no less. Stuckler's main example is a political-science paragraph that packed three debates (effectiveness, long-term peacebuilding, legitimacy) into five sentences. He called it "this wall of abstraction" and fixed it by splitting it into three paragraphs, one per point, each with its own evidence.

## PEER

- **P — Point.** The topic sentence. It states the paragraph's one claim.
- **E — Evidence or example.** A number, a study, a case.
- **E — Explain.** What the evidence means and why it supports the point.
- **R — Repeat / link.** A closing sentence that settles the point and leads into the next paragraph.

## Skip test

Read only the first sentence of every paragraph. The argument should still be easy to follow. If it isn't, fix the outline before touching any sentences.

## Keep related concepts together

Put the cause next to its effect and the timing next to what it describes. Stuckler's example: "complex instructional materials reduce student performance during the intervention phase" puts the timing next to the wrong noun. The fix is "complex instructional materials during the intervention phase reduce subsequent student performance."

## How AI edits break meaning (the most useful part for AI-assisted writing)

When Stuckler had ChatGPT tighten sentences, the prose got leaner, but the meaning changed in four ways:

1. **Synonym swap:** "consumption" became "intake". The two terms differ in some fields.
2. **Dropped scope:** "interventions aimed at reducing sugar consumption" became "reducing children's sugar intake". The claim went from being about interventions to being about sugar.
3. **Changed verb strength:** "reduce" became "harm". That is stronger than the evidence supports.
4. **Widened outcome:** "metabolic outcomes" became "metabolic health".

The `write` skill's meaning-drift check (Step 5) looks for exactly these four changes.
